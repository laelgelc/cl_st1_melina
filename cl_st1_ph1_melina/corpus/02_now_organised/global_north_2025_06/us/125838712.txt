<p> Forty years ago , undergraduates occupied one of the signature buildings at prestigious Brown University , the John Carter Brown Library . Nobody got tear-gassed . Changes occurred . The student action , and administrative response , stand as a lesson for our own , extremist moment . <p> Thomas Hallock SUMMERVILLE HEADSHOTS Courtesy of Thomas Hallock <p> The Brown activists , Organization of United African People , laid out demands that today seem reasonable , even mainstream . They wanted more faculty of color ; a multi-cultural curriculum ; diversified admissions and financial commitment to building a broader student body ; an African-American Studies Department , and build-out of the campus Third World Center . <p> They chose the perfect spot to occupy . The JCB ( as this revered institution calls itself ) holds down a corner of the campus ' central academic green . This Beaux-Art , hilltop temple houses one of the world 's great collections of Americana , spanning all of North and South America , purchased from the residuals off molasses and the slave trade . Pressure to divest financially from apartheid South Africa set university President Howard Swearer at odds with fiscally conservative alums . ( This was Ronald Reagan 's second term . ) Hunger strikers would starve themselves in the college chapel . Frat boys drew national headlines for holding up scorecards at female pedestrians . Activist Amy Carter faced a high-profile expulsion . <p> When undergraduates took over the JCB , the administration first threatened arrest . The savvy protesters had brought their homework , however , and called the university 's bluff . What would be the charges , they asked -- arrest students for studying in a library ? <p> Then the administrators did something remarkable : They listened . <p> And to their credit , administrators adopted a plan for change . Back in the 1980s , when applying to colleges , I was not Brown material myself .
