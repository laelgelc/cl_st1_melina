These policies fuel unimaginable levels of violence , turning the border into an ugly and dangerous laceration . <h> Defending the Color Line <p> Racist violence has always distinguished the immigration bureaucracy and its deterrence strategies . During World War II , the INS even swept 120,000 Japanese Americans into internment camps , dramatizing the racist logic driving immigration policy . In a decision with inescapable symbolism , the INS recycled wire from one internment camp to construct a wall along the U.S.-Mexico border in 1945 . <p> The historian Adam Goodman reveals that officials favored deterrence policies that made deportation a uniquely wrenching experience . For years , the INS packed immigrants into squalid and overcrowded boats headed for southern Mexico , in order to make the trip as long and traumatic as possible . An official investigation in 1956 justified the inhumane conditions , claiming that Mexicans lived " in general not much better than animals . " Ship deportations ended after passengers on the Mercurio mutinied that year , igniting a public scandal for both countries . <p> Undocumented crossings peaked after the Hart-Celler Act of 1965 established quotas on immigration from the Western Hemisphere . Rather than a defensive barrier , the border wall was a weapon in a war against refugees . <h> Reluctant Allies <p> Ultimately , the refugee crisis inspired a wave of restrictive legislation . The INS monitored the region with night vision equipment , flood lights , electronic sensors , and other cutting-edge hardware .
