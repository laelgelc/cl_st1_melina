<h> Unesco chair Alison Phipps 10 things that changed my life <p> Academic and Writer Professor Alison Phipps photographed at . Glasgow University where she is Professor of Languages and Intercultural Studies <p> Adam Robertson spoke with Unesco chair for Refugee Integration Alison Phipps on the 10 things that changed her life . <p> 1 Growing up in Thatcher-era Sheffield <p> THIS was during the stationing of cruise missiles at Greenham Common and during the closure of all the steelworks and mines in that area . Politically , it really educated me greatly . <p> I think through that , I grew passionate about nuclear disarmament and injustice . I saw the consequences of mass unemployment and the devastation it created for the people in my classes . <p> There were suicidal levels of despair amongst people , particularly young people . And I think that , in many ways , set me on a path that meant I was always concerned with injustice and politics . <p> In my lifetime , it was the most destructive legacy of a politician . <p> The city has never recovered and has @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ and ability to thrive in well-sustained communities was devastated . <p> It was tuppence anywhere in the city on the bus , you could go anywhere . It was an amazing trade union city , famously calling itself the socialist republic of South Yorkshire . <p> But all the things that came with Thatcher 's legacy , the devastation of the Falklands War and the cynical ways that happened made it really clear to me that there was an injustice here being perpetrated against ordinary people . <p> 2 Learning French and German <p> I WENT on a school exchange to France and Germany when I was 14 and it changed my life . It showed me there was a way out of what was a really depressed environment in the city . <p> It showed me cultural diversity , linguistic diversity . I subsequently studied languages , I lived abroad and have learnt many languages since then . <p> Travelling in languages and having recourse to how different people understand the world has been constantly life-changing . <p> I never fail to be surprised by words in any language @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ different languages and switch to maybe one that is n't from home but is a different way of expressing myself . <p> They 've become love lines in my life . I have different people I care about dearly who speak in a different language to my own and my life is infinitely enriched by those ways of being . <p> And that 's led me to begin to learn Gaelic since living in Scotland and want to engage with the languages of this land . On Burns Night , I can now understand poems in Scots . <p> 3 Being taught names of flowers , trees and hills <p> MY mum and dad taught me this and through this I knew that I was indigenous to this lovely , denuded land of ours . I think that was a way I knew I was the daughter of people who loved the world and wanted to share it with me . <p> I think it was a lesson in humility but naming all the hills round about where I grew up , all the names of the plants , particularly @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ to love that and have an active knowledge of plant lore and plant life that was laid down at an early age . <p> I can name and recognise many , many species . It 's linked to learning languages but you get to learn names from different areas as well . <p> I came to appreciate them very much and they told me I 'm from these lands . <p> 4 Romeo And Juliet <p> WHEN I was 14 , my mum took me to see Romeo And Juliet at Chesterfield Theatre performed by The Young Vic . <p> It was performed with a cast of black actors as the Montagues and white actors as the Capulets . It completely changed my life . <p> It was the ultimate education in race relations , it helped me understand race riots , it helped me understand the futility of warring factions . It was an absolutely brilliant introduction into what would be a life of anti-racist advocacy . <p> It showed me the power of the arts to bridge the gap between the " other " , to enable us @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ know it as an absolute tragedy because this does n't need to happen . <p> It placed me in a position where I wanted to do something . I saw the tragedy and futility of culture wars and wars in any form but particularly around racism . <p> Sheffield was quite a white city when I was growing up there , rather like Glasgow until about 20 years ago , but it sensitised me very deeply and they are key themes in my work . <p> I went into the theatre one person aged 14 and came out a different person . <p> 5 Lessons in hospitality <p> THIS is quite a personal one . The lessons , particularly in hospitality , of my grandparents and my parents of the community I grew up in and of the church parish changed my life . <p> I think one of those was learning to still myself in prayer and habits of prayer . I think through that , yes there might have been what we could call a faith laid down , but far more importantly was an ability to give @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ gratitude in my life . <p> I think the ability to sit in silence and in stillness is one which has soothed me in very difficult times . Just the ability to sit with it and sit in silence and hold it in the context of powers greater than myself . <p> 6 Moving to Scotland <p> I MOVED with my husband Robert when we were very newly married . We came to Glasgow which for me , coming from Sheffield , was very much a home from home . <p> It was one trade union city to another , one city devastated by the poll tax and the closure of the shipyards to another trying to regenerate as a city of culture . <p> ( Image : newsquest ) <p> One of the first things I did was start volunteering to visit Dungavel Immigration Removal Centre with Scottish Detainee Visitors . <p> Through that , with Positive Action in Housing , back in I think about 2005/06 , we shared our home with people sharing asylum which we 've done for many years . <p> Those relationships that I @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ to live with us , all of them have changed my life profoundly and for the better . <p> 7 Getting an allotment <p> IT 'S within walking distance of my house and I 've become a tenant of the land . I think that connected me back to that sense of indigeneity -- the ability to grow my own vegetables , to never have to go to a supermarket , to harvest my own potatoes , make my own jam and care for the land . <p> I think through that practice , the land cared for me . It provisioned me and my family and all those folk living in my home . <p> I think allotments are important , radical spaces of gatherings , of being neighbours -- we leak into each other as allotment holders , share our produce and practice a common life that is important . It can often be fractious ; we learn conflict transformation around whether a foxglove is a beautiful , wild flower or a weed . All of that I think has been transformational . <p> 8 Becoming a member @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Christian community , also an interfaith community that has about 300 members . <p> It 's worldwide and we have a rule of life which is about a commitment to justice and peace and care for the Earth . <p> I recommit to that every year but that practice of life is a non-violent , conflict transformational , peace-loving community and being part of that community and part of structures of accountability for how we share the Earth 's resources , our money , how we live our lives and having a group of elders to go with deep wisdom has been something for which I am always grateful . <p> And becoming a member has certainly changed my life . <p> 9 When we fostered our daughter Rima <p> SHE turned up on our doorstep as an unaccompanied minor from Eritrea . We ended up in a long struggle with the Home Office for her right to be recognised as a refugee . <p> Through that , I became a strange , Eritrean-style mother but also a grandmother now and discovering , particularly as a grandmother , there 's @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the love of the grandmother for the grandchildren is just the most amazing thing and I had no idea what that would be like . <p> That was probably the most life-changing moment of my life was when Rima arrived on the doorstep . <p> 10 Working with Keith Hammond <p> MY colleague Keith Hammond , a brilliant philosopher , invited me to begin working with him in Palestine , especially in the Gaza Strip . <p> That was 15 years ago and that has always been a life-changing experience but no less so than in the last 15 months of the genocide . <p> And the most life-changing thing I think about all of it is the courage and determination of my colleagues in Gaza to continue their education , to continue living life and to refuse to have their dignity made destitute by Israel and the Israeli illegal occupation . <h> Why are you making commenting on The National only available to subscribers ? <p> We know there are thousands of National readers who want to debate , argue and go back and forth in the comments section @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ readers in Scotland , asking each other the big questions about the future of our country . <p> Unfortunately , though , these important debates are being spoiled by a vocal minority of trolls who are n't really interested in the issues , try to derail the conversations , register under fake names , and post vile abuse . <p> So that 's why we 've decided to make the ability to comment only available to our paying subscribers .
