" It 's my way of keeping our heritage alive , one bite at a time , " she says . <p> Maamoul 's lightly sweet dough is made from semolina flour , sugar and ghee , then rolled into discs and folded around a pliable , sweetened mixture of either pistachios , walnuts or dates to make a round biscuit . Some bakers use tools to hand-press a decorative pattern into each biscuit -- Khalaf presses her maamoul into wooden moulds , and a different shape is used for each filling . <p> " My grandmother and mother taught me to make maamoul . It was always a family event . We 'd gather in the kitchen , kneading the dough , stuffing each piece carefully . As a child , I loved trying to mimic the shapes my mother made . " The dough should be soft yet sturdy enough to hold the filling , and the filling should be just the right sweetness to complement the delicate flavour of the dough . Patience and love are the key ingredients . " <p> In Palestinian homes , Eid-al-Fitr -- the days of feasting following the holy month of Ramadan -- are when sweets like maamoul and kaak are typically made , but Khalaf says versions of the biscuits can be enjoyed all year long . <p> " I remember coming home from school , tired and a little grumpy , especially during exam season , " she says . " As soon as I stepped inside , the smell of butter and cinnamon would hit me . Mama had made kaak . It was n't the Eid kaak filled with dates , but the plain , round , sesame-crusted ones . She 'd hand me one still warm from the oven with a glass of sweet tea and we 'd sit together .
