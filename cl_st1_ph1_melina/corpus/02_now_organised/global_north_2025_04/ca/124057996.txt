<h> Personal Perspective : In crisis , meditation and interconnectedness may heal us . <p> In our increasingly interconnected world , the weight of global events can feel crushing . It 's not uncommon to experience a profound sense of unease , even physical symptoms , when confronted with news of suffering and upheaval . I recently experienced this firsthand during my daily routine at the YMCA . Upon hearing about the suspension of aid to vulnerable women and children in Gaza , I was struck by a sharp chest pain . While thankfully not a heart attack , my doctor suggested it might be " broken heart syndrome , " or stress-induced cardiomyopathy . <p> This experience led me to reflect on the broader implications of collective trauma . While " broken heart syndrome " is often associated with individual loss or grief , research suggests it can also manifest on a societal level . Studies have shown a rise in Takotsubo cardiomyopathy cases following large-scale traumatic events like 9/11 and the COVID-19 pandemic . It 's as if our shared psyche is absorbing the collective pain , leading to a widespread feeling of broken heart syndrome . <p> In today 's world , a mood of retribution and payback seems to be on the rise . Care for vulnerable populations is diminishing , and fundamental human values like empathy , tolerance , and mercy are being challenged . In such a landscape , our hearts struggle to thrive . They may shrivel , harden , or even break under the weight of upheaval . <p> But there is hope . The good news is that the symptoms of a broken heart , whether individual or collective , are often temporary . Our hearts possess an incredible capacity for healing and resilience . This requires a two-pronged approach : inner and outer healing practices . <p> For inner healing , we can find refuge from the world 's chaos and pain . Meditation is a tool we can use to access a deeper , more grounded state of being . By relaxing into a space of awareness and warmth , we can connect with something beyond our human turmoil . <p> In this deeper ground , we can gently acknowledge our fears and sorrows without being overwhelmed by them . As we rest in this space , our stress can gradually dissolve . John Makransky , of Boston College , calls this the " field of care , " a space where we can allow our vulnerable feelings of anxiety , dread , and grief to surface and be welcomed . By acknowledging and processing these emotions , we can untangle the painful knots within us and rediscover our capacity for presence and engagement . <p> Meditation is not just about inner peace ; it also inspires compassionate action .
