Bedraggled after years of wearying culture war battles over no-platforming and cancellation , we find ourselves confronting strange new threats on every side . At the weekend , The Times reported that the police have been arresting as many as 30 people a day on the bewilderingly trivial grounds that they have sent messages causing " annoyance " , " anxiety " or " inconvenience " . They presumably include the two parents detained in a cell for 11 hours for posting " disparaging comments on WhatsApp " and sending " a high volume of emails " to their children 's school . People are much keener on the idea of it for themselves than for their enemies . Support for free speech turns out all too often to be founded not on high principle but self-interest . Free expression is often favoured by those who have most to gain from it . It was a left-wing cause in the 1960s when the conservative establishment seemed impregnable . But when the power to censor language was seized by progressives , it became a right-wing hobby horse . Now in America , as the right regains cultural power it has rapidly abandoned much of its recent enthusiasm for the cause of tolerance . <p> Dispiritingly , intolerance seems to run deep in human nature . The brief postwar flourishing of libertarian attitudes to speech between the end of the Chatterley ban and the first WhatsApp arrests may come to seem merely a blip . <p> Future historians may reflect that free speech owed its moment of ascendancy less to destiny than to the vagaries of circumstance . Crucial to the story was the fact that as America and Europe faced down the Soviet Union in the Cold War , freedom of speech was celebrated as a " western value " , a crucial point of difference with the totalitarian east .
