<h> Bios <h> The islands that went from whale hunting to whale watching <p> From dramatic humpback breaches to enthralling dolphin acrobatics , a springtime visit to the Azores is synonymous with incredible sightings of whales and dolphins . <p> The remote Portuguese archipelago , consisting of nine volcanic islands about 900 miles west of Lisbon , lies in the North Atlantic Ocean , putting it on the migration route of several whale species . And from March to June -- whale migration season -- lucky tourists may even catch a glimpse of behemoths like the blue whale and the fin whale , the two largest animals on the planet . <p> As well as migrating whales , the Azores also have resident cetaceans , including sperm whales and some dolphin species , which can be seen year-round . With nearly a third of the 94 known cetacean species in the world observed here , the archipelago consistently ranks as one of the best places on the planet to go whale watching . <p> But the centuries-old relationship between Azoreans and whales has not always been so harmonious . The Americans recruited Azorean men at low pay , enticing them with the offer of residence in the US once they 'd worked a certain number of years on the ships . Many emigrated to Massachusetts with the whalers . <p> By the middle of the 19th century , some of those emigrants returned to the archipelago , bringing with them the knowledge of whale hunting and dedicated boat construction . They kicked off the local whaling industry , building seven-man hunting canoes and equipment . Soon , factories processing whale oil , meat and bones sprung up on the islands . <p> Whaling seems unspeakably cruel from a modern perspective , but back then it was a matter of survival . After crude oil was discovered in 1859 , the demand for whale oil decreased dramatically in the following decades . <p> Right until the industry 's end , in 1987 , the practices of Azorean whalers were different from the factory ships of the Americans . They continued to hunt in their small wooden boats with handmade weapons , killing only a small number of sperm whales . The last whale was said to have been killed in 1987 by some disgruntled whalers from Pico island . <h> New beginnings <p> The transition away from whaling gave birth to new industries and practices -- with the impetus coming from outside .
