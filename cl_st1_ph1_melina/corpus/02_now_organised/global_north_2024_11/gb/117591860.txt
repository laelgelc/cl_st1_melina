<p> Perhaps it 's inevitable that , as the full-scale invasion of Ukraine approaches its third anniversary , attention has begun to wane . With the emergence of new crises from Gaza to Sudan , the world seems to be finding it difficult to stay focused on a war that may now have claimed , per US estimates , nearly one million casualties , yet has become depressingly normalised . <p> This state of affairs , however , only makes the publication of The Language of War ( Allen Lane , ? 18.99 ) , a work of reportage-cum-memoir by Oleksandr Mykhed , even timelier . Mykhed , one of Ukraine 's leading writers and critics , is serving in the armed forces , and his topics range from refugees abandoning their pets to the meaninglessness of religion in a time of genocide . " If a Russian mine tears off limbs , " Mykhed writes , " then there are no legs to reach the church . Plokhy elegantly recounts the occupation of the power-plant in February 2022 : held hostage by Russian troops , the plant foreman managed to turn the situation on its head , informing the Russian occupiers that in a place as contaminated as this , they would have to follow the rules . The Russians knew they had to survive , Plokhy writes , " if they were to survive " . <p> Olena Stiazhkina takes us even further back .
