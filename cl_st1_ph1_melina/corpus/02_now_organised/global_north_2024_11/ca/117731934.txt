<p> In 1937 , a ship left Nazi Germany for what was then called Mandatory Palestine with a secret cargo . Inside a shipping container marked " lumber " sat a stack of elaborately painted wood panels depicting Jerusalem and Jewish life in a Bavarian town . The painted sides faced each other , artwork concealed . To inspectors ensuring nothing of value was taken by Jews fleeing Germany , they would have looked like a pile of wood . <p> Those 30-odd panels assembled into a temporary dwelling , a sukkah , that for nearly a century had been an annual fixture outside Naftali and Zili Deller 's homestead in Fischach , Germany . For the seven days of Sukkot , the Jewish harvest festival , it hosted guests and family meals . <p> Many observant Jews erect sukkahs for Sukkot . The Dellers ' had paintings commissioned from an artist in 1826 . Among few to survive the Holocaust intact , it is now in the Israel Museum in Jerusalem .
