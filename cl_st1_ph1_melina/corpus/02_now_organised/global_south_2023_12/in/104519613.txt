<h> Jhumpa Lahiri on being an outsider everywhere , telling the other side of the Roman story in her new book Roman Stories and the importance of defending free speech <p> In Well-Lit House , one of the most poignant stories in Jhumpa Lahiri 's new collection , Roman Stories ( Penguin Hamish Hamilton , Rs 499 ) , after years of a hard-scrabble life in immigrant camps and shared accommodations , a young man shores up at an apartment of his own in a low-income neighbourhood on the outskirts of Rome with his wife and five children . It never becomes home though . Everyday xenophobia from the predominantly Italian neighbourhood forces his wife -- who wears a veil -- and children to leave . " Go wash those dirty legs , " scream two young men on a motorino , in another story , " The Delivery " , after shooting at a dark-skinned young woman out to collect a parcel for her employer . <p> The Pulitzer-winning writer has always had a special relationship with Rome -- it is where she found love and a new language ; where she charted an entirely new course in @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the Rome she portrays in this collection of nine stories , ( six of which she has translated herself from the original Italian to English . The other three have been done by Todd Portnowitz ) is more noir , less romantic . It is the story of nameless foreigners -- outsiders -- trying to find a foothold in a city that is lonely and less than inviting , sometimes even openly , brazenly hostile , to them . In this interview , Lahiri , 56 , speaks of this Rome of light and shade , her ambiguous relationship with borders and why she has stopped using names for her characters .
