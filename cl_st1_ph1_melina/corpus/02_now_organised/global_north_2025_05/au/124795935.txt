<p> Topic : Australians take a lot of pride in their preferential voting system , which is predicated upon the belief that there 's no such thing as a wasted vote . But is that true ? Is the 2025 federal election result -- a thumping Labor mandate , a Coalition decimation and a Greens wipeout -- a fair and proportional outcome ? <p> Question : Does Australia 's voting system suck ? <p> Debaters : Writer Robert Lechte is arguing the affirmative . Election analyst William Bowe is arguing the negative . <p> Democracy sausages ! Preferential voting ! Compulsory voting ! An independent electoral commission ! The Australian democratic tradition is a source of widespread national pride and patriotic sentiment . Particularly for a certain demographic of educated , ABC-watching , lower-case-L liberals , election day represents Australia at its finest . <p> Longtime ABC election-night host Antony Green has become a living embodiment of this feeling . Not only does he explain the machinery of Aussie democracy to the nation on election night , but he also personifies it and its unique merits . And the Antony Green personality cult is the biggest cultural obstacle to recognising this and fixing it . <p> The core principle of democracy is " one person , one vote " . Voting being a quantitative exercise , we can measure how well a voting system lives up to this ideal by measuring proportionality . If every vote counts equally , then the final allocation of seats ought to be in proportion to the number of votes . This is simple arithmetic : twice the votes , twice the seats . Half the votes , half the seats . <p> By this measure , Australia 's system is a disaster , particularly if we consider this latest federal result . The ALP received roughly 2.86 times the primary vote ( aka , first preferences ) of the Greens , but will finish with at least 93 seats to the Greens ' 1 seat . While preferential voting does mean that all votes participate in the final head-to-head " runoff " on each seat ( as they should ) , there 's a larger problem it does n't solve : single-member electorates . <p> Because single-member electorates are winner-takes-all , losing votes do n't get any representation at all in the final allocation of seats . The winner gets all the seats available : one . This is what drives " knife-edge " dynamics where large numbers of seats hinge on very small percentage shifts . <p> It 's also what leads parties to focus their campaigning entirely on a few close swing seats , at the expense of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ a losing seat has no effect on the result . The same is true for adding further winning votes in already very safe seats . <p> By adding up losing and excess winning head-to-head votes in each seat , you reach a percentage of " wasted votes " . These votes are conceptually granted no representation at all in parliament . Remember , this is head-to-head votes , after preferences are redistributed , so this does n't even include the problem of one 's vote not going to a candidate that is one 's first preference , as would be the optimal outcome . <p> Let 's take the seat of Macnamara , for example . After preferences were redistributed to the two frontrunners , the ALP 's Josh Burns won with 55,795 votes to Liberal Benson Saulo 's 34,664 . How many votes actually receive representation ? The 34,664 losing votes obviously do n't get representation -- Benson Saulo lost and wo n't be in parliament . This favours major parties and parties with voters concentrated in certain electorates ( " teals " , the Nationals , etc ) , while punishing parties with more dispersed voters . <p> The Senate does n't solve this problem . It also lacks true proportionality due to smaller-populated states being over-represented relative to larger ones ( a similar albeit less extreme situation to the US Senate , where Wyoming and Montana receive the same number of senators as California and New York ) . Voting in New Zealand requires one piece of paper and ticking two boxes ( only one of which usually matters ) . Australia requires two pieces of paper ( one of them comically large ) and a lot of numbering , which in combination with compulsory voting and language difficulties , leads to large numbers of invalid votes , particularly in areas with high language diversity . The primary vote had ALP up 2% , Coalition down 3.5% , Greens unchanged .
