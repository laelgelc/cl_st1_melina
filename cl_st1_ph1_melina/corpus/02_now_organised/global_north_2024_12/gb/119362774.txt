<h> Will Israel bomb Iran -- and 3 more questions for the world in 2025 <h> Whether it 's China , Ukraine , Trump or the Middle East , Britain will have to tread carefully , a former foreign policy adviser in No 10 writes <p> John Bew <p> Saturday December 28 2024 , 3.55pm , The Sunday Times <p> It is likely to be a pivotal year in the making or breaking of the modern world , with profound consequences for the United Kingdom . <p> Indeed , it is hard to think of a period in postwar history in which foreign policy has the potential to alter the lives of people in this country so dramatically , from their bank bills to their personal security . <h> Hard choices <p> Kyivites take shelter in a subway station during an air raid alert on Christmas Day <p> THOMAS PETER/REUTERS <p> Since Russia 's invasion of Ukraine , we have seen the growing alignment of our adversaries around the world -- Iran , Russia , China and North Korea -- and their increased willingness to support each other with diplomatic cover , sanctions evasion , lethal weaponry , technological subsistence for their defence industrial bases and now , in the case of North Korea 's intervention against Ukraine , the sending of troops to fight and die in each other 's wars . <p> In response , a quantum leap is required in the way we reimagine , re-energise and reinforce the alliances that have long been the strategic trump card of the democratic world . <p> But this is also an era of hard choices . What we ca n't afford is sentimentalism for an era that is past . In the race to innovate and adapt , there will be no salvation in wistful paeans to the old liberal international order . <h> Advertisement <p> There are several big swells in the ocean that have the potential to crest and come crashing down upon our heads in the year ahead . <p> The first , which remains the most important to the UK , is the future of Ukraine . We can be certain of some things in 2025 . One is that every actor , including presidents Zelensky and Putin , is gearing up for some sort of big political move and positioning themselves to maximise their leverage beforehand . <p> Yet the gulf between the general sense that a deal is coming and the creation of the conditions to bring it about remains huge . The problem with the idea of a " land for peace " deal , in which Ukraine cedes Russian-held territory in return for an end to hostilities , is that Putin 's primary target is not territory alone : it is the Ukrainian self-determination and sovereignty that would cement its place in the western orbit . <p> In the short run , then , the UK should be piling everything it can on to Ukraine 's side of the ledger in a " no regrets " strategy for 2025 , whether a viable peace negotiation emerges or not . Already , the prospect of putting some western troops in parts of Ukraine , albeit only in a training capacity or in the context of a ceasefire , is being actively considered in several European capitals . And there is now a live debate in both the EU and US Congress on how the hundreds of billions of dollars in frozen Russian assets , of which only the profit has been shaved off so far , could be used to strengthen the hand of Ukraine ahead of any talks . <p> If the UK wants to steer the Trump administration towards maintaining support for Ukraine , part of the answer is to show that Europe is willing to put more skin in the game itself . <h> Advertisement <h> Will Israel attack Iran ? <p> An anti-Israeli billboard looms larger in Tehran <p> ATTA KENARE/AFP/GETTY IMAGES <p> The second coming dilemma , and the one that may come at us fastest , is the next chapter in the Middle East conflict that has been raging since October 7 , 2023 , but has simmered away for a decade or more prior to that . <p> This war has many dimensions , including ongoing Houthi attacks on shipping , the future governance of Gaza and the attempt to find a stable equilibrium in post-Assad Syria , where Turkey is arguably the main beneficiary and Israel is already on the ground . <p> But the main event is likely to be what happens next between Israel and Iran , where the taboo on inter-state conflict -- direct attacks on each other 's countries -- has collapsed in the past 12 months . <p> Iran 's theocratic regime was by far the biggest geopolitical loser of 2024 . Its proxies and patsies , from Hamas to Hezbollah to Bashar al-Assad , have suffered a series of stunning reverses . Could it now accept a deal in which its nuclear ambitions are visibly and dramatically curtailed ? Or will it opt instead for a game of brinkmanship against an Israel that has shown , notably with its airstrikes in October , that it can hit the regime at its heart , damaging Iranian air defences in the process ? <p> With the arrival of Trump , Israel may now have a partner in the White House that is willing to back it in going further in exerting maximum pressure on Iran , potentially even targeting its nuclear capabilities . There is no playbook for how the UK would respond in such circumstances , but one can foresee a hard dilemma about whether to align with a ratcheting-up , or to try to sit it out . <h> Advertisement <h> The third dilemma <p> President Xi inspects troops this month in Macau , southern China <p> LI GANG/XINHUA/AP <p> The China challenge is rising back up the agenda just as Rachel Reeves prepares to travel to Beijing and the government seeks to improve its trading relations . Tensions have once again been rising in the South China Sea and over Taiwan , around which China conducted its largest military exercises in decades in early December . <p> For many years , national security officials have warned about a theoretical Taiwan invasion scenario taking place around 2027 , which is when Xi Jinping has ordered his military to be ready to take the island . But this single event horizon , the subject of a thousand war games , may not be the scenario that presents itself . <p> There is growing evidence that China could instead be adopting an incrementalist approach to change the facts on the ground in real time , asserting its control over territorial waters via its coast guard and navy , and gradually eroding Taiwanese sovereignty . <p> This type of asphyxiation strategy may seem less dramatic , but could very swiftly have a cascading effect on many other areas of our national security . Because Taiwan is the world 's main source of advanced semiconductors , which power our smartphones and laptops , this would deliver a shock to supply chains and the global economy that would make Ukraine pale by comparison . <p> Again , there is no playbook for Britain here . But there may be lessons from the case of Ukraine , when there was a divide in the G7 between those who wanted to advertise their economic deterrence measures before a potential invasion and those who thought such measures would be politically viable only once a Russian invasion actually took place . <h> Advertisement <p> Finally , there are those strategic challenges that governments like to bracket as " transnational " . Of these , migration and energy security are foremost . Policies on both will be defining issues at the next general election . <p> Yet , just as important , the government will also need to act fast to develop a new national security agenda for AI -- one that builds on but goes beyond the successful AI Safety Summit at Bletchley in 2023 -- if it is to stand any chance of keeping pace with the world . <h> The Trump factor <p> Donald Trump is Time magazine 's person of the year for 2024 <p> ALEX BRANDON/AP <p> The coming Trump administration is already brimming with a new elite of doges from the tech world who will surge efforts to win the race for technological primacy over China and spur American growth , unapologetically burning up a lot of rocket fuel and other energy sources on the way . <p> Meanwhile , the government is feeling its way towards a relatively modest reset with the EU , with Sir Keir Starmer establishing a new Whitehall unit to strengthen ties with the Continent . But this could be complicated by the prospect of a new form of 21st-century trade deal with the US . <p> There are two views in the US of how Trump 's international economic policy might play out . One version is a zero-sum and brutal use of tariffs to correct trade deficits and give a short-term fillip to domestic manufacturing . <h> Advertisement <p> But the other nascent proposition , seemingly favoured by Trump 's treasury secretary nominee Scott Bessent , is that America could create something like a US-led economic and security commons , bringing close allies such as the UK and Australia closer to its orbit in areas like tech regulation . <p> If the latter approach begins to crystallise , it will be difficult for Starmer 's government to resist the advances of the American grizzly bear . <p> The prize would be considerable , but a price will be exacted . <p> Voters expect Sir Keir Starmer to be an active part of the new world , John Bew writes <p> ANDY RAIN/EPA <p> Reaching a favourable deal with the US will not be possible if the UK diverges significantly on issues like China or indeed Israel , the issue on which all elements of the Trump administration are united and where the distance between London and Washington may be most pronounced . <p> And what would it mean for the EU reset if the UK had a sweetheart deal with the US , in which it was excluded from tariffs crashing down on the rest of Europe ? <p> The electorate will expect the government to be active participants in this new world , rather than passive recipients of whatever it throws upon our shores . Could Starmer be the first leader to see President Trump in 2025 , perhaps even before his inauguration , with a bold proposition for how the US-UK relationship could be bolstered on trade and security ? <p> There is a lot to be said for first-mover advantage in foreign policy . But the path ahead requires imagination , boldness , risk-taking and a willingness to accept difficult trade-offs that would strain any government , particularly one dealing with sclerotic growth . <p> One thing is for certain , though , in the era of grizzly bears , tech pioneers and new frontiers : playing possum wo n't do . <p> John Bew is professor of history and foreign policy at King 's College London .
