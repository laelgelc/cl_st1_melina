<h> How to survive Christmas with your family , by the experts <h> An archbishop , a divorce lawyer , a hostage negotiator and others dispense advice on how to avoid falling out over the festive period <p> Take a breath before becoming angry and tell yourself " all is well " , a family therapist recommends <p> The Sunday Times <p> Sunday December 22 2024 , 12.01am , The Sunday Times <p> The royals are not the only ones who face an awkward festive lunch : loved ones might be at loggerheads , but peace can be brokered if you follow our experts ' advice . <h> Prepare to be annoyed <p> Julia Samuel , family therapist <p> If your Christmas has the familiar hotspots that drive you crazy -- the unreconstructed family member who says all the wrong things at the wrong time , the one who drinks too much or the angry person who intermittently sends missiles across the table -- prepare yourself for the usual clashes by rehearsing a different response to well-trodden arguments . <p> Jot down on a piece of paper the people and types of behaviour that ignite a negative response in you . Fully envisage what happens and imagine what your response might be , and get as angry as you like . <p> Then envisage a different scenario , where they do the annoying thing but you react differently . Perhaps you take a breath , pretend to go to the loo , leave the room , walk around or get a glass of water . Tell yourself : " I am safe , I am calm , all is well . " Go over this a number of times until you can really imagine your averting , calming behaviour . <h> Advertisement <p> On the big day , rehearse again and think of a treat you can give yourself when you have achieved your goal . Choose a great one , not a stingy one . I use this technique a lot at Christmas and it has certainly got me out of a few dinner party disasters . <h> Walk in their shoes <p> Rowan Williams , former Archbishop of Canterbury <p> Our convictions do n't come from nowhere . We grow into what we believe -- because of argument and information , yes , but also because of who we trust , who we spend time with , what resonates , what brings back important images from our past and so on . <p> One technique for handling painful conflict at Christmas could be saying : " Tell me a bit about how you came to believe this , and why it came to have the importance it does . " <p> Thinking back to some quite sharp conflicts in my time as archbishop -- conflicts with implacably conservative colleagues in Africa , for instance -- part of my own learning was realising how easily a disagreement could feel like another top-down directive from a more privileged to a less privileged person . It 's important to have time for this to be named and explored . <h> Advertisement <p> It never helps to talk as if my opinion was so obvious that no sane person could disagree . That , in turn , means being willing to talk about how I too have learnt or grown . No guarantee of final convergence but perhaps each of us may look just a bit more three-dimensional to one another . <h> Lay a place for ' the judge ' <p> Thomas Haggie , divorce lawyer <p> Inviting to the Christmas lunch table a neutral figure , who will review what is said and done , has a powerful effect on the temperature . <p> In divorce cases , this is often the judge . " What will the judge think ? " people wonder , as they proof-read a nasty letter . They delete some adjectives . They take out poisonous remarks . They present their best selves . <p> A lonely friend or neighbour invited to Christmas might have a similar effect , as it did one year for us . Adult children will become conscious of , and embarrassed by , a reversion to teenage stroppiness . The stranger might just be interested in what everyone has to say , oblivious to the swirling currents beneath . Not being on home turf , they will probably be polite . Everyone has a better chance of being their best , or least bad , self . The risk of a full-scale bloodletting , however satisfying , is reduced . <h> Advertisement <p> But it is also a win-win : if your guest does behave badly , or try and stick the knife into someone , there is nothing like a common enemy to pull everyone together . <h> Respect the taboos <p> Suzanne Williams , hostage negotiator and associate fellow at Oxford University <p> An important part of a negotiator 's preparation includes " do-not-mentions " . When I was working with the British government on hostage releases in Iraq and Gaza , there would always be subjects you knew not to bring up . <p> Likewise , at Christmas , you may wish to note topics to avoid at the table . Themes such as politics or a family indiscretion may be on your list . If these subjects are accidentally raised and tensions run high , apologise , de-escalate and do n't raise them again . <h> Advertisement <p> On one occasion in the Central African Republic , an innocent mistake had a negative impact . Someone misheard a compliment and interpreted it as an insult against his family . It triggered a huge emotional outburst and it took some time to get the negotiations back on track , nearly destroying the relationship . <h> Laugh , and the world laughs with you <p> Al Murray , comedian <p> When things get tense around the Christmas dinner table , as they are bound to do , using humour to defuse a situation can be not unlike defusing a bomb , the chief difference being that when you cut the red wire instead of the blue wire and are blown to kingdom come , you do n't get to say , pleadingly : " I was joking . " And any joke that requires explanation can not a joke be , surely ? <p> So , keep a bit of levity in your back pocket , especially if you 're hosting and the gravy 's gone claggy , the turkey 's on fire and Auntie Shirley 's had too much port . I tour an awful lot , and if we did n't laugh at the sheer tedium of closed service stations and a chronic lack of phone reception , they might get us down . Make this your iron law and the world gets easier : if it 's laughable , laugh at it . <h> Advertisement <h> To thine own self be true <p> India Knight , columnist and author <p> There 's a certain point in life when you have to decide to spend Christmas in a way that makes you happy . Christmas should n't be about tiptoeing round annoying or objectionable people , even if they are family members ( mine are all heaven , of course ) . But I think the idea of the seasonal-fixture racist uncle ranting away at the end of the table needs to be put to rest . Do n't invite him . If nobody likes spending time with him , that 's his problem . It 's certainly not the problem of everyone else who is just trying to have a nice day . <p> So , my advice is that if the idea of a big family Christmas brings on a headache , do n't have that kind of Christmas . Decide who you actually want to spend time with , and do that . I feel that older people , who have smiled fixedly through family dramas for several decades while simultaneously producing feasts for vast numbers of people , have really earned the right to say : " Do you know what , you 're all exhausting . We 're spending the day at home in our pyjamas eating Turkish delight . " <p> If opting out is n't an option , it helps to refuse to be allocated your childhood role within the family even though you are a grown adult . All families do this : he 's the funny one , she 's the chaotic one , he 's lazy so there 's no point in asking him to help , she 's unlucky in love etc . It 's reductive and really pushes people 's buttons , so try not to do this to anyone , let alone yourself , unless you look back on your childhood family dynamics as having been exceptionally golden and harmonious .
