<p> New laws protecting free speech on campus are likely to be given the green light in the new year . <p> However , the Higher Education ( Freedom of Speech ) Act -- shelved when Labour won the general election -- is expected to be watered down from the proposals first set out under the Tory government . This could remove the right for cancelled speakers to seek compensation . <p> The act was designed to strengthen existing laws by putting a duty on universities and student unions to actively promote as well as protect free speech -- and it would have left them liable to being sued by aggrieved parties . <p> It also created a new role of director of free speech within the Office for Students ( OfS ) , the independent regulator of higher education in England , giving it the power to deal with complaints and penalise universities or student unions for breaches . The Cambridge philosopher Arif Ahmed was appointed to the job but has kept a low profile . Lord Wharton of Yarm , a Conservative peer who retained the Tory whip and was a campaign manager for Boris Johnson , stood down as OfS chairman within days of the election . <h> Advertisement <p> Sources say that ministers were taken by surprise at the strength of feeling when the act was put on hold , as it had been widely criticised when going through parliament and was amended by the House of Lords . They believe it will now proceed but discussions about revisions are continuing . <p> There was an outcry from hundreds of academics and high-profile campaigners , including Stephen Fry , AC Grayling and Richard Dawkins , against it being axed . An open letter said that academics and students had been hounded , censured , silenced or even sacked over the past 20 years for expressing legal opinions . <p> A Gaza protest outside UCL in May ; the act was due to force universities to promote as well as protect free speech <p> GUY BELL/ALAMY <p> The Free Speech Union , led by Toby Young , an associate editor of The Spectator , is bringing a judicial review at the end of January to challenge the decision to suspend the act . <p> Under discussion is the removal of the " statutory tort " element that would allow those who felt they had suffered loss from their free speech being impinged to bring civil claims for damages against universities or student unions . <p> There had been opposition among some MPs and universities to the statutory tort , which , it was feared , could lead to institutions being overwhelmed by vexatious or time-wasting complaints . <h> Advertisement <p> The tort was removed by the House of Lords but reinstated in the Commons before the bill was enacted . It is thought this could again be removed from the act . <p> A source said that Ahmed was still in place at the OfS and working on strategy but there was a degree of uncertainty until decisions about the act were made . " You could still have a complaints system without the tort , " the source said . " There would be a bunch of amendments around that . All options are open for government . " <p> However , the Office for Students has been told by the Department for Education to focus on the financial problems faced by universities and this week said it would have to put much of its other work on hold . <p> Another higher education policy figure said that Ahmed had been silent despite being brought in to champion free speech : " He got the job because he 's so outspoken but he 's been the quietest man in public life . " <p> They added : " People in government were taken aback by the rearguard action to save the act . The tort aspect could be withdrawn but that may remove the teeth from the act . It risks upsetting everyone , by adding red tape to universities but not going far enough for those who want it to be tougher . " <h> Advertisement <p> David Willetts , the former universities minister , was among those with concerns about the initial draft of the bill as it progressed through parliament , particularly the tort aspect . He said : " While I support the legislation overall , I had concerns about the threat of litigation actually deterring a university from inviting outside speakers at all . " <p> The act would still have an impact if the tort was removed , he said . Instead , the OfS could fine universities or use a range of other powers . <p> Ministers were now faced with the same problem as their predecessors in drawing up draft guidance , he said . " I would be very surprised and disappointed if the legislation did n't happen at all , I hope they 're trying to find a way forward . " <p> Grayling , the philosopher and author , said he supported removing the tort element . " Universities should be very robust in defending free speech but it would be wrong to give provocateurs a right to sue -- that will be an invitation to them to stir up trouble and get a payoff for it -- and wrong to fine universities or student organisations for opposing those they dislike .
