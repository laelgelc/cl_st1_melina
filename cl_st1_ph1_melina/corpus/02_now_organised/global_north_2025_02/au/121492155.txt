<h> ' Truth ' , a play about Julian Assange , is exhausting . That 's the point <p> We live in a post-WikiLeaks world . We now take it for granted that , say , the horrors occurring in Gaza will be shared with us , independent of official messaging . On the other hand , the surveillance state feels so entrenched it rarely seems to elicit more than a shrug from the general public . <p> It 's difficult to remember how revolutionary the moment was when , around the turn of the last decade , WikiLeaks first came to prominence . The use of technology to shift the disparity of information access between governments and the governed had the potential to create a genuinely new world . <p> At its strongest points , Patricia Cornelius ' new play , Truth , which opened at Melbourne 's Malthouse Theatre this week , reinforces that principle : that what WikiLeaks sought was not simply revelation for the sake of it , or holding power to account ( though this was certainly part of it ) . They jet around a single , sparse set that features five desks with early desktop computers , a back wall made up of five cages , a flurry of CCTV cameras and , above those , 12 connected television-sized screens . The brisk , polemic show rattles through Assange 's story , the actors rhythmically trading lines and occasionally meeting in unison like an acapella group . <p> This conceit is frankly a bit naff at times , particularly in a clumsy opening segment dedicated to Assange 's school days . The actors take turns imitating schoolyard bullies , calling the young Assange -- already smarter and less capable of compromise than anyone else he knows -- a freak and a weirdo . It successfully transported me back to my school days , except it was conjuring the experience not of being bullied but of being at assemblies watching an overzealous theatre troupe perform a godawful play about tolerance . <p> Things improve greatly once we @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ where he finds human connection , limitless possibilities and problems worthy of his intellect and ambition . Then comes US Army Private Chelsea Manning , who hands over the hundreds of thousands of documents hat will come to be known as the Afghanistan War logs , the Iraq War logs and " Cablegate " . <p> " Collateral Murder " , the most notorious of WikiLeaks ' revelations , is played nearly in full on the fixed screens above . The footage fades and that silence lingers . <p> Then we get to Sweden . Assange is " drunk on success " , parties hard in between seminars and meetings and , upon leaving for the UK , finds out via the media that he is wanted for questioning in relation to allegations of rape and sexual assault . <p> The inclusion of the women 's experiences is handled well , as well as it can be . What we know of their stories and how we know it is deftly and uncomfortably sketched . We are told of the torturous conditions Manning ( played by Eva Rees ) is subjected to as she starts serving what will eventually be seven years of a 25-year sentence . Indeed , if anything , I found myself noting the places it could have gone further . Assange mentions at one point he has been abandoned by the Australian government ; he could have added that he was also largely abandoned by the media who had fed happily on his revelations ( and handily avoided the kind of consequences he faced ) . Manning 's segment is absolutely harrowing , but again , could have gone further -- after years of imprisonment , she attempted suicide , for which she faced further charges , the subject of a hearing at which she was granted no legal representation , nor witnesses . <p> As the play presses forward all zeal and momentum , it 's not always clear that it has total control over its own contradictions . Whether it is the atrocities committed in our name , but without our knowledge , or the prison conditions faced by whistleblowers , Truth reminds us again and again : We can not say we did n't know .
