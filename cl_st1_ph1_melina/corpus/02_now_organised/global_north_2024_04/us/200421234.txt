Diament <p> Opinion contributor <p> Jews will gather Monday for the Passover Seder as we have for centuries . Children are taught to ask four questions with the opening , " Why is this night different from all other nights ? " But this year , there is a fifth question American Jewish families will ask at their Seder table : " What is our place today in America ? " <p> This year , Jews will celebrate Passover in the shadow of Hamas ' Oct. 7 attack on Israel - the bloodiest day for Jews since the Holocaust - and amid the largest surge in antisemitism ever in the United States . <p> As we celebrate our freedom from Egypt , we will think about the Jews who are hostages in Gaza , taken captive solely because they are Jews . " A teenager will turn to a grandparent and ask : " Are we watching a replay of 1930s Germany here ? " <p> These questions will be asked by Orthodox Jews , who are acutely tuned to the patterns of Jewish history , which tells of " golden ages " that have come and gone for centuries . <p> And these questions will be asked by liberal Jews , who might have reservations about how Israel has prosecuted its war against Hamas but have even greater reservations about their erstwhile friends and colleagues siding with Hamas . <p> These Jews have fought on @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ , economic , women 's , LGBTQ+ and other rights . That answer will depend on the American people and how we , collectively , right our course and respond to this decisive moment in American Jewish history . <p> But the Passover story makes one thing clear : In the age-old fight between good and evil , good will triumph . <p> There 's a reason the Passover Seder is the most observed Jewish ritual across the full spectrum of Jews . It holds a message of promise that the Jewish people will endure in the face of unimaginable evil . Great empires - Greek , Roman , Ottoman and British - have come and gone , but we Jews remain . <p> Non-Jews gravitate toward the Passover story for the same reason .
