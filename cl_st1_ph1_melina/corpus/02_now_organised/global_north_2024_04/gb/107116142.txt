<p> IT 'S Humza Yousaf 's first year as First Minister and the public domain is full of unflattering , half-hearted and unflinching assessments . <p> Whilst conceding that Yousaf 's term has been overshadowed by the seemingly endless police investigation of Nicola Sturgeon , the general ranking has been six or seven marks out of 10 . Is that fair ? Is it good enough ? <p> One nippy comment in The National described Yousaf as " the archetypal nice guy , someone with a CV more suited to head up a third sector , international charity than lead a nation to freedom " . <p> It 's a sair fecht when apparent honesty and integrity are negative qualities , but also evidence that very different yardsticks are at work when it comes to measuring the FM 's performance . <p> " Nice guys " may succeed in the limited devolved reality of Holyrood , but can they " cut it " in the muscular struggle for independence ? <p> Most folk will acknowledge that Yousaf is a natural conciliator whose strong and early call for a ceasefire in Gaza also acknowledged the suffering in Israel . <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ an urgent ceasefire ( embraced by Westminster parties five long months later ) he was also visiting synagogues in Scotland and encouraged a joint statement by imams and rabbis working together for community cohesion . <p> All this as his in-laws were trapped in Gaza , uncertain each day if they would live to see the next . <p> It would have been easy to be angry , righteous or retreat completely from public life . Yousaf struck none of these pointless poses and won respect from almost every quarter , enabling his Westminster lieutenant Stephen Flynn ( below ) to make the same principled stand in the Commons . <p> Ironically the issue of Gaza -- completely outwith the competence of the Scottish Parliament -- has won more plaudits for Scotland , Holyrood and the SNP and more focus on the party 's second-class status at Westminster than anything else . <p> There 's a lesson in there for any SNP First Minister . Do n't be constrained by the devolution settlement . <p> Outspoken on Gaza , Yousaf is generally more of a conciliator . And though ( grudging ) acceptance by councils has been chalked up as another success for Yousaf , the inevitable cuts in council services will be laid at his door . <p> Generally though , Yousaf has emerged from a year @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ negotiate . <p> The trouble is that 's not how most voters rate this First Minister . Firstly , the absence of strikes is indeed a mercy , but also quite hard to savour -- especially when network broadcasters make inaccurate , sweeping statements that do n't acknowledge the very different climate of industrial relations in Scotland . <p> Secondly , for every low-key domestic policy " win " there 's a high-key domestic policy failure , like the vexed issue of ferry construction . Sure , all fingers now point to Caledonian Maritime Assets Limited ( CMAL ) rather than CalMac or the Scottish Government for producing ferry specifications that were impossible to deliver . <p> But ferries owner CMAL is a wholly owned public corporation of the Scottish Government , with Scottish ministers as sole shareholders . So where does the buck stop ? <p> Why did no-one notice that not a single " reality-proofing " islander was on CMAL 's board until a massive fuss erupted last year ? In short , no change since 2012 . <p> Why is Scotland the only country in northern Europe without any form of land tax ? Why has the SNP not fulfilled its 2007 SNP pledge to reform council tax ? Because they are both difficult ? <p> I 'm afraid that 's the inescapable conclusion and the core of dissatisfaction with Humza , his party and the whole Scottish Parliament -- tackling the relatively " soft " social stuff , instead of the hard economic stuff . <p> Take the Hate Crime Act -- controversial but passed by a cross-party consensus of 82 MSPs . <p> Its passage has quietly taken years of parliamentary time and effort , whilst citizens have struggled on with crippling financial burdens and our economy , land and industrial base have remained captured by overseas companies and multinationals . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Sarwar ( below ) had the cheek to hint at this in a recent speech ; " Holyrood has overseen sweeping social change " , but , " we have been very much a social policy parliament , rather than an economic policy parliament . I wonder which party stood in the way of devolving real economic powers in 1997 . <p> It 's not fair to expect sweeping economic change from a fiercely constrained devolved parliament -- but life 's not fair . And Scots do n't just expect it -- they are craving it . <p> Can the Scottish Parliament get Scotland out of the British economic rut or is Holyrood only able to mitigate the cruellest benefit " reforms " and legislate for behavioural change ? <p> Andrew Tickell analysed this conundrum in an excellent Sunday National article pegged to news that Holyrood plans to outlaw pet abduction . <p> " Increasingly , the limits of devolution seem to be channelling proposals like this through parliament -- not necessarily because they reflect real priorities @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ within legislative competence .
