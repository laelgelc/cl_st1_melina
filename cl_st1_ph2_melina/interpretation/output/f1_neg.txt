## Interpretation of f1 (negative pole)

### Preferred label: **Human vulnerability and contested postwar futures**

This pole appears to gather discourse about the human and institutional aftermath of the Gaza war: bodily deprivation, healthcare needs, hostage suffering, ceasefire implementation, and competing plans for Gaza’s reconstruction or redevelopment. The pole is not highly lexically unified, however, and the label should therefore be treated as broad and provisional.

### Lexical evidence

The clearest coherent cluster in the loadings concerns **physical vulnerability and humanitarian provision**:

- *healthcare, emaciate, dehydration, inadequate, desalination, meal, vaccine, dose, bodily*
- Less directly, *kneecap* also evokes bodily injury.

These words point to reporting on malnutrition, thirst, damaged services, medical care, vaccination, and bodily harm. This humanitarian strand is reflected in the examples through references to:

- hostages appearing **emaciated**—a derivational echo of the loading *emaciate*, although it was not counted as an exact loading-word match;
- Gaza’s decimated health infrastructure;
- an infant dying from hypothermia;
- mass displacement and extensive Palestinian deaths;
- the physical treatment and deaths of hostages.

A second cluster suggests **reconstruction, commodification, and mediated visions of Gaza**:

- *redevelopment, yacht, dirham, selfie, newsfeed, advertisement, Oscar, epic*

This is especially compatible with the articles about Trump’s AI-generated “Gaza Riviera” or “Gaz-A-Lago.” Those texts frame Gaza’s possible redevelopment as a digitally circulated spectacle involving luxury resorts, cryptocurrency, NFTs, tourism, and real estate. The combination of *redevelopment* with luxury and media vocabulary therefore supports a discourse of **commercialised or spectacularised postwar reconstruction**.

A weaker third cluster is institutional or procedural:

- *requalify, exam, seeker, misidentification, unintentional, trespassing, impartiality, blackmail*

These items may occur in legal, administrative, educational, migration, or accountability reporting. This has some correspondence with the examples on a hate-crime trial, university appointments, campus discipline, and negotiations over hostage–prisoner exchanges. Nevertheless, this cluster is too heterogeneous to sustain a narrower institutional label by itself.

Several loadings—such as *nication, sev, lim, ing,* and *gat*—look truncated or otherwise noisy. Other items such as *tola, chef,* and *lawn* have no evident common discourse in the excerpts. The pole consequently contains a substantial residual or topic-specific lexical component.

### Evidence from the excerpts

The examples, all drawn from `global_north_2025_02`, concentrate on four interconnected February 2025 developments:

1. **Hostage deaths, return of bodies, and collective mourning**, particularly the Bibas family;
2. **Ceasefire and exchange negotiations**, including Palestinian prisoner releases;
3. **Material and bodily consequences of war**, including emaciation, hypothermia, damaged healthcare, and displacement;
4. **Contested futures for Gaza**, especially Trump’s proposed takeover and AI-mediated luxury-redevelopment imagery.

Other examples concern the war’s **domestic and transnational repercussions**: campus protests, Palestinian Studies appointments, antisemitism claims, anti-Palestinian violence, and disputes over the legitimacy of Hamas. These establish an additional discourse of institutional and ideological conflict, but it is much more prominent in the excerpts than in the loading list. It should therefore be treated as a contextual strand rather than the pole’s defining meaning.

Importantly, the extraction reports **no exact negative-pole loading words in these excerpts**. This substantially weakens any claim that the examples directly instantiate the lexical basis of the factor. Their relationship to the loadings is mainly thematic or derivational. The pole is thus better interpreted as a loose constellation of aftermath-related discourses than as a single sharply defined register.

## Groups driving the pole

Because this is the negative pole, the relevant groups are those with the **lowest mean Factor 1 scores**. All group means are positive, so “negative” here denotes relative location on the factor rather than groups having negative average scores.

The lowest means are:

- `global_north_2025_02`: **2.35**, N=815
- `global_south_2023_09`: **2.50**, N=10
- `global_north_2025_03`: **2.66**
- `global_north_2025_04`: **2.71**
- `global_north_2024_05`: **2.79**
- `global_north_2025_01`: **2.82**
- `global_north_2025_05`: **2.88**
- `global_south_2025_02`: **2.89**

The very small `global_south_2023_09` stratum should not be treated as strong evidence because it contains only ten texts. Among the well-represented groups, `global_north_2025_02` is the clearest driver and also supplies every example. Its relatively low SD of 2.22 suggests that its low mean is not solely produced by a few extreme articles.

The broader pattern indicates an **early-2025 concentration**, especially in the Global North from January through May. The Global South also moves relatively close to this pole in February–April 2025, although generally less strongly. This timing plausibly corresponds to the January 2025 ceasefire, hostage and prisoner exchanges, the return of bodies, debates about the next phase of negotiations, and Trump’s February redevelopment proposal.

By contrast, the pole is relatively less characteristic of many mid-to-late-2024 groups, particularly Global South groups in August–October. This may reflect changing historical salience—from active-war reporting toward ceasefire consequences and postwar planning—but it could also reflect differences in topic mix and sampling. The uneven group sizes, despite IQR capping, require caution in treating this as a simple geopolitical distinction.

## Other possible labels

1. **Humanitarian precarity and postwar reconstruction**  
   Closely reflects the strongest loading clusters, especially medical deprivation and redevelopment. It underrepresents the hostage and institutional material in the examples.

2. **Bodily harm, hostage aftermath, and Gaza reconstruction**  
   More closely captures the February 2025 examples, but “hostage” itself is not supported by the loading vocabulary and may overfit the limited excerpt set.

3. **Mediated aftermath and commodified reconstruction**  
   Highlights the conjunction of human suffering with AI imagery, social-media circulation, luxury redevelopment, and cryptocurrency speculation. This works particularly well for the Trump-Gaza examples but is too narrow for the whole pole.

4. **Fragmented humanitarian and post-conflict current-affairs discourse**  
   The most cautious label. It acknowledges that the factor combines humanitarian, legal-institutional, media, and redevelopment vocabulary without forming a fully cohesive semantic domain.

Overall, **Human vulnerability and contested postwar futures** is the most defensible label because it accommodates both the humanitarian loading cluster and the excerpts’ concentration on hostage suffering, ceasefire implementation, and rival visions for Gaza after the war, while not overstating the pole’s lexical coherence.
