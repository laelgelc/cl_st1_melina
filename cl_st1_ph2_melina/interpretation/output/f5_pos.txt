## Preferred label

### **International legal adjudication of genocide and preventive state obligations**

This pole encodes the **juridification of the Gaza conflict**: events are represented through international courts, treaty law, formal allegations, evidentiary thresholds, provisional remedies, binding obligations, and questions of implementation or enforcement. Its most prototypical realization is reporting on South Africa’s genocide case against Israel at the International Court of Justice (ICJ).

## Justification

### 1. Loading structure

The loadings form a highly coherent international-law register:

- **Judicial institutions and procedure:** *court, judge, jurisdiction, proceeding, case, prosecutor, warrant, verdict, ruling, advisory, submit*
- **Legal authority and duties:** *legal, law, international, convention, obligation, binding/non-binding, enforce, implementation, violation*
- **Genocide law and evidentiary classification:** *genocide, genocidal, prevention, incitement, persecution, intent, plausible, allegation, accusation, crime, humanity*
- **Interim protection and remedies:** *provisional, measure, irreparable, safeguard, unhindered*
- **Institutional and diplomatic enforcement:** *resolution, veto, abstention, sanction, export, license, mechanism*

The highest loadings—*irreparable* (.91), *ethnical* (.88), *provisional* (.85), *omission* (.82), *bodily* (.80), *jurisdiction* (.76), and *prevention* (.72)—largely derive from formulaic legal formulations. Phrases such as “irreparable harm,” “provisional measures,” “acts and omissions,” “serious bodily harm,” and “national, ethnical, racial or religious group” are characteristic of court applications, treaty definitions, and judicial rulings.

Thus, the pole is not simply about condemnation or descriptions of wartime suffering. It frames suffering and military conduct as matters to be **legally classified, adjudicated, prevented, and remedied through international institutions**.

### 2. Evidence from the excerpts

All ten high-scoring excerpts come from **global_south_2024_01** and focus on South Africa’s ICJ application, the January 2024 hearings, or the Court’s provisional-measures ruling. They repeatedly reproduce the pole’s core lexical bundles:

- “Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide”
- “provisional measures”
- “irreparable harm”
- “acts and omissions”
- “genocidal intent”
- “international legal obligations”
- “jurisdiction”
- “prevent and punish…incitement to genocide”
- “submit a report to the court”

The discourse is strongly procedural. The articles explain:

1. what South Africa alleged;
2. whether the ICJ had jurisdiction;
3. whether Palestinian rights under the Convention were “plausible”;
4. what interim measures the Court could order;
5. whether those orders were binding or enforceable; and
6. how long a final determination might take.

The excerpts also show that this pole accommodates **contested legal argumentation**, not only the assertion that genocide has occurred. Several texts report South Africa’s allegations alongside Israel’s rejection of them as “baseless,” “distorted,” or lacking the required *intent*. Terms such as *plausible, allegation, accusation, reasonable,* and *intent* point to the evaluation of legal thresholds and competing submissions. The pole therefore concerns **legal accountability claims and adjudicative reasoning**, rather than a single unqualified political position.

A secondary component is **protective humanitarian legality**. Orders concerning unhindered humanitarian assistance, basic services, preservation of evidence, and prevention of irreparable harm cast humanitarian access as a treaty-based obligation and judicial remedy.

### 3. Groups driving the pole

The strongest mean is:

- **global_south_2024_01: 6.08** (N=385, SD=6.46)

This is substantially above all other group means and directly corresponds to the South Africa–Israel ICJ hearings and provisional ruling. The high SD suggests considerable within-group concentration: January 2024 Global South coverage was not uniformly legalistic, but some articles used this register very intensively, as the example scores of 21–28 demonstrate.

Other prominent groups are:

- **global_south_2024_11: 5.40**
- **global_north_2024_11: 4.38**
- **global_south_2025_06: 4.20**
- **global_south_2024_12: 3.96**
- **global_south_2025_04: 3.87**
- **global_south_2024_05: 3.83**
- **global_north_2025_06: 3.51**
- **global_north_2025_04: 3.47**

The joint North–South rise in November 2024 is consistent with renewed attention to international judicial accountability. The loading inventory’s *warrant, prosecutor, court,* and *jurisdiction* indicates that the full factor extends beyond the January ICJ case to other court-centred developments, likely including international criminal proceedings and arrest-warrant coverage. Likewise, *resolution, veto, abstention, sanction,* and *export/license* broaden the pole toward institutional mechanisms for implementing or obstructing legal accountability.

### 4. Geopolitical and temporal tendencies

Global South means are higher than Global North means in most matched months, especially:

- January 2024: **6.08 vs 3.41**
- November 2024: **5.40 vs 4.38**
- December 2024: **3.96 vs 3.38**
- June 2025: **4.20 vs 3.51**

This cautiously suggests that Global South reporting more strongly or more frequently foregrounds Palestine/Gaza through **international law, genocide conventions, court proceedings, and state accountability**. South Africa’s role as applicant also gives this discourse particular Global South salience. The excerpts include South African, Indian, Nigerian, and Pakistani reporting, alongside discussion of African support and precedents involving Gambia and the Rohingya.

Nevertheless, the pole is clearly transregional: the North and South both rise in November 2024, and Global North means become relatively high in parts of 2025. It is therefore better interpreted as a globally circulating legal-institutional discourse with somewhat stronger Global South realization, not as an exclusively Global South formation.

The temporal profile is event-sensitive rather than a simple linear increase. There is:

- a major peak in **January 2024**, linked to the ICJ case;
- a lower but continuing legal discourse through mid-2024;
- another marked peak in **November 2024** across both geopolitical regions;
- renewed strength in **April–June 2025**.

These fluctuations probably reflect the timing of court decisions, warrants, hearings, diplomatic enforcement debates, or related legal developments. Because group sizes and variances differ—and September 2023 has especially small samples (North N=25; South N=10)—the contrasts should not be treated as pure geopolitical effects.

## Other defensible labels

1. **International judicial accountability and genocide-law discourse**  
   Emphasizes courts, legal responsibility, genocide classification, and accountability mechanisms.

2. **Court-mediated genocide claims and provisional protection**  
   Captures the especially strong ICJ vocabulary of allegations, jurisdiction, plausibility, provisional measures, and irreparable harm.

3. **Juridification of Gaza through international law**  
   Highlights the transformation of military and humanitarian events into legal cases, treaty obligations, rulings, and enforcement questions.

4. **International-law remedies, compliance, and enforcement**  
   Gives greater prominence to *obligation, ruling, enforce, mechanism, veto, sanction,* and *implementation*, although it is somewhat less specific to the dominant genocide-law cluster.

Of these, **International legal adjudication of genocide and preventive state obligations** best combines the pole’s dominant genocide-convention vocabulary with its procedural, remedial, and compliance-oriented character.
