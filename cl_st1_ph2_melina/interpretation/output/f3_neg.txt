## Recommended label

### **Ceasefire administration, hostage exchange, and post-war governance discourse**

This label best captures the combination of institutional, procedural, and operational reporting associated with the negative pole. The examples concentrate on the February 2025 ceasefire: hostage–prisoner exchanges, the transfer and identification of remains, negotiations over subsequent phases, ministerial statements, and proposals for Gaza’s future governance or redevelopment.

### Evidence from the excerpts

All ten examples come from **global_south_2025_02**, the group with the lowest mean Factor 3 score (**0.761**), and all have individual negative scores. They repeatedly cover:

- implementation or possible extension of the ceasefire;
- releases and exchanges of hostages and Palestinian prisoners;
- the Bibas family, the return of bodies, and their identification or **misidentification**;
- negotiations mediated by the United States, Qatar, and Egypt;
- statements by prime ministers, deputy foreign ministers, ambassadors, and other officials;
- Trump’s displacement and **redevelopment** proposal;
- future governance, reconstruction, and humanitarian implications.

The recurrent tagged loading word is **minister** (-.35), occurring through references such as *Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu*, *Deputy Foreign Minister Sharren Haskel*, and Gaza’s *deputy minister of agriculture*. This supports an institutional and governmental register. Other loading items visibly represented in the excerpts include **misidentification** (-.42), **finalise** (-.31), **redevelopment** (-.38), and **humanitarian** (-.31). These words point respectively to forensic disputes, negotiated implementation, plans for post-war Gaza, and the management of civilian conditions.

The examples are largely event-driven news reports. Even where they contain moral evaluation—grief over the hostages, criticism of displacement, allegations of censorship, or calls for Muslim solidarity—the reporting is organised around official decisions, agreements, handovers, investigations, and policy proposals.

## Contribution of the full loading set

The complete loading list broadens the interpretation beyond the February 2025 examples. Several lexical clusters are apparent:

1. **Official diplomacy and political authorization**
   - **minister, peacekeeper, abstention, finalise, Israeli-backed, US-backed**

2. **Humanitarian access and material provision**
   - **humanitarian, flour, unhindered, unload, GHF**

3. **Reconstruction and territorial planning**
   - **redevelopment**

4. **Technical conflict operations and incident investigation**
   - **wireless, hand-held, walkie-talkie, detonation, unexploded, misidentification, unintentional, suspiciously, Taiwanese**

The fourth cluster is especially prominent among the stronger loadings and likely indexes detailed reporting on communications devices, explosions, weapons, or sabotage investigations. It is not well represented in the supplied excerpts, so the pole should not be reduced to ceasefire reporting alone. Taken together, the loadings suggest a broader discourse of **institutionally managed conflict logistics**: the practical administration of negotiations, exchanges, aid, reconstruction, security incidents, and official responses.

Some high-loading forms—**sev**, **nication**, **then-foreign**, **ing**, and **tion**—appear truncated or extraction-dependent, while words such as **chariot, selfie, yacht, tola, dirham**, and **epic** do not form a clear Gaza-specific semantic cluster. These items lower confidence in a narrowly thematic interpretation. The coherent institutional, humanitarian, and technical clusters should therefore carry more interpretive weight than isolated or malformed forms.

## Groups driving the pole

Because this is the negative pole, the relevant groups are those with the **lowest** mean scores. No group mean is actually negative, so this is a relative association: the pole appears in individual texts, but it does not dominate any monthly group as a whole.

The lowest-scoring groups are predominantly Global South strata:

- **global_south_2025_02: 0.761**
- **global_south_2023_10: 0.825**
- **global_south_2023_12: 0.854**
- **global_south_2024_03: 0.884**
- **global_south_2025_01: 0.937**
- **global_south_2024_01: 0.945**
- **global_south_2024_02: 0.972**

The examples strongly identify **Global South February 2025** as the immediate driver. That month coincided with the first phase of the January 2025 ceasefire, repeated hostage–prisoner exchanges, the Bibas identification dispute, and intensified debate over Trump’s Gaza redevelopment and displacement proposals. The semantic concentration may therefore reflect genuine historical salience.

More generally, Global South means are lower than corresponding Global North means in every listed month. This suggests that the negative end is relatively more associated with the sampled Global South reporting. However, this should not automatically be interpreted as a stable geopolitical style: group sizes vary, the excerpts come from only one month, and the full loading set includes technical incident vocabulary not illustrated in those examples.

There is also a temporal pattern. Low Global South scores occur through late 2023 and early 2024, reach their minimum in February 2025, and then rise sharply in March–April 2025. This may indicate that the pole was particularly salient during discrete stages of ceasefire implementation and official crisis management rather than following a simple linear diachronic trend.
