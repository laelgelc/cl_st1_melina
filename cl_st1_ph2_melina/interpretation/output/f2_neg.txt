## Interpretation of f2 (negative pole)

### Preferred label  
**Digitally packaged episodic institutional news about diplomacy, mobility, and conflict administration**

This pole appears to encode a relatively heterogeneous news mode in which Palestine/Israel is reported through discrete institutional developments: diplomatic recognition, visa and travel rules, border and permit regimes, government appointments or procedures, and individual conflict incidents. A secondary component is the digital packaging surrounding such reports, including newsfeed language, promotional material, and subscription boilerplate.

### Evidence from the loadings

The most semantically coherent loading cluster concerns **formal institutions, diplomatic relations, and regulated mobility**:

- *then-foreign, consular, reciprocity, waiver, airline, govt*
- *inauguration, nominee, constitutional, oval*
- *Arab-American, flotilla*

These terms suggest reporting about foreign ministers, government transitions, visa reciprocity, consular access, travel, and state policy. This interpretation is directly supported by the September 2023 examples concerning:

- possible Pakistani recognition or normalisation of relations with Israel;
- Saudi Arabia and the Abraham Accords;
- Israel’s admission to the US Visa Waiver Program;
- the treatment of Palestinian and Arab-American travellers.

The phrase *on the cusp* appears in the normalisation reports, while *then-foreign* identifies former foreign ministers involved in earlier diplomatic contacts. *Nication* is probably a tokenisation or OCR fragment of *communication*, rather than an independent discourse marker.

A second loading cluster points to **online-news infrastructure and miscellaneous newsfeed content**:

- *newsfeed, promotional, advertisement, opt-out, ticket*
- *selfie, yacht, button*

This cluster is visible in the February 2025 Fox News excerpts, where *promotional* and *opt-out* occur in newsletter subscription notices appended to stories. It indicates that this pole is partly shaped by publication templates and bundled webpage material, not solely by the semantic content of Palestine/Gaza reporting.

Other items—such as *requalify, submarine, Swedish, Taiwanese, doctoral,* and *postdoctoral*—are difficult to integrate into a single Palestine-related discourse. They suggest that the pole also captures heterogeneous peripheral topics or source-specific lexical residue. The strongest loading, *newsfeed* (-.90), reinforces the possibility that document format and aggregation practices are important.

### Evidence from the excerpts

The excerpts collectively contain several related institutional and episodic discourses:

1. **Diplomatic normalisation and recognition**  
   The two clearest negative-scoring September 2023 texts discuss whether Pakistan might establish relations with Israel, Israeli claims about further Muslim-majority states normalising relations, and the diplomatic repercussions of contacts with Libya.

2. **Visa reciprocity and unequal mobility**  
   Two articles report Israel’s entry into the US Visa Waiver Program while foregrounding concerns about discrimination against Palestinian and Arab-American travellers. Here, Palestine is framed through administrative access, reciprocal rights, and security exemptions.

3. **Administrative restriction of Palestinian life**  
   Other September texts concern Gaza export bans, border closures, work permits, medical permits, and restrictions on trade and movement. These reports frame occupation and blockade through their institutional and socioeconomic effects.

4. **Routine incident reporting**  
   Several excerpts report raids, deaths, arrests, border protests, and official casualty totals in the West Bank and Gaza. Their style is event-centred and heavily attributed to ministries, armies, international organisations, and news agencies.

5. **Later US-centred controversy reporting**  
   The February 2025 examples cover hostage remains and campus protests, often through strongly evaluative US media framing. Yet their directly attested negative-pole vocabulary is mainly the appended newsletter language—*promotional* and *opt-out*. This makes them evidence for digital packaging at least as much as for a coherent substantive discourse.

The correspondence between the loading list and the excerpts is therefore uneven. Several examples contain no identified f2-negative loading words, while the clearest lexical matches occur in the diplomatic-normalisation and website-boilerplate texts. The pole should consequently be treated as a **mixed discourse-and-publication-format dimension**, rather than as a sharply unified ideological position.

## Groups driving the pole

Because this is the negative pole, the relevant groups are those with the **lowest mean scores**, even though all reported group means are positive. The strongest relative associations are:

- **global_south_2023_09:** 4.30, but only \(N=10\)
- **global_north_2025_02:** 4.76, \(N=815\)
- **global_south_2025_02:** 5.10, \(N=498\)
- **global_north_2024_09:** 5.45, \(N=689\)
- **global_north_2024_08:** 5.77, \(N=768\)
- **global_north_2025_03:** 5.75, \(N=815\)
- **global_north_2024_07:** 6.00, \(N=749\)

The September 2023 Global South group supplies most of the early examples and fits the pole’s diplomatic and pre-October-7 institutional agenda. However, its mean is based on only ten texts, so its extremity is highly sensitive to sampling. The corresponding Global North September 2023 group is also small (\(N=25\)).

The more reliable temporal signal is the **February 2025 trough in both regions**, especially in the capped Global North group. The February 2025 excerpts suggest a shift toward US domestic controversy, hostage-centred reporting, and publication-specific promotional material.

There is also a broader geopolitical tendency: **Global North means are lower than Global South means in every directly comparable month**. Thus, this negative pole is relatively more characteristic of Global North strata overall. Nevertheless, the gap should not automatically be interpreted as a purely geopolitical discourse difference. It may also reflect outlet composition, syndicated content, webpage extraction practices, or residual imbalance across national sources.

Temporally, Global North scores decline through July–September 2024 and again around February–March 2025. Global South scores also reach a marked low in February 2025, but otherwise remain generally higher. These patterns may reflect changing news agendas and historically salient events, but the mixed lexical structure prevents a more specific diachronic claim.

## Alternative labels

1. **Institutional diplomacy, mobility regulation, and episodic conflict reporting**  
   Best if emphasis is placed on the substantive examples and the coherent policy-related loadings.

2. **Administrative access and international-relations news**  
   Highlights normalisation, visas, permits, crossings, exports, and official state procedures.

3. **Peripheral newsfeed and institutional miscellany**  
   A more cautious label that recognises the large number of unrelated loading terms and the importance of *newsfeed*, promotional boilerplate, and bundled webpage content.

Overall, the first label is the most inclusive, but the third may be methodologically safest: f2-negative appears to capture both a substantive institutional-news discourse and source- or webpage-specific lexical noise.
