## Interpretation of f9 (negative pole)

### Preferred label
**Personalised Israeli hostage victimhood, recovery, and mourning**

This pole encodes highly personalised reporting on Israelis abducted during the 7 October attacks, particularly Nova festival and kibbutz victims, their captivity, recovery or identification, and subsequent communal and familial mourning. It combines accounts of violent abduction and death with biographical details that restore victims’ individuality and with demands to bring the remaining hostages home.

### Evidence from the loadings

The loading set contains several closely connected lexical clusters:

- **Captivity and abduction:** *captivity, captive, kidnap, reunite, handover, unaccounted*
- **Recovery and investigation:** *recover, discovery, forensic, rescuer, responder, paramedic, tunnel*
- **Violent death:** *murder, behead, shooting, grenade, explode*
- **Mourning and commemoration:** *funeral, grief, heartbroken, coffin, vigil, condolence, memorial, synagogue*
- **Family and personal relationships:** *parent, daughter, girl, female, love*
- **Victims’ lives and leisure identities:** *music, festival, concert, soccer, football, joy, entertainment*
- **Specific 7 October settings and people:** *kibbutz* and especially *gat*, referring to hostage Carmel Gat.

Together, these loadings suggest more than general reporting about hostages. They represent a **human-interest and commemorative discourse** in which captives are identified through families, hobbies, gender, age, and places associated with their abduction. Words such as *music, festival, concert, soccer,* and *football* are important because they reconstruct victims as ordinary, socially embedded individuals rather than treating them only as casualty statistics.

The very strong loading of *gat* (-.80) also indicates that the pole is partly event- and person-specific. It is strongly associated with coverage of Carmel Gat and the five other hostages whose bodies were recovered from a Rafah tunnel in late August 2024. Some loadings—*button, subscribe, promotional,* and *opt-out*—are website or newsletter boilerplate and should not be treated as substantive discourse features.

### Evidence from the examples

All ten examples come from **global_north_2024_09** and focus on the recovery of six hostages’ bodies, especially Hersh Goldberg-Polin and Carmel Gat. The repeated narrative sequence is:

1. abduction from the Nova **music festival** or a nearby **kibbutz**;
2. prolonged **captivity**;
3. discovery and **recovery** from a Gaza **tunnel**;
4. claims that the hostages were **murdered** shortly before Israeli troops arrived;
5. **funerals, vigils, memorials, condolences, grief,** and public protest;
6. appeals for a ceasefire-hostage agreement to return those still alive.

The examples repeatedly use the pole’s loading words in emotionally and narratively central positions. For instance, the funeral coverage includes *funeral, parent, condolence, murder, recover, daughter, love,* and *tunnel*. Other texts combine *captivity, grief, kidnap, memorial,* and *synagogue*. This produces an affective discourse of bereavement and collective trauma.

The victims are also extensively biographised. Hersh Goldberg-Polin is described as a lover of music, soccer, football, geography, and travel; Carmel Gat is associated with compassion, rock concerts, and family life. Other victims are described through partners, children, occupations, educational plans, and acts of assistance during the attack. This confirms that **personalisation and memorialisation**, not merely hostage-status reporting, are central to the pole.

Several examples additionally connect grief to political accountability. Families and protesters argue that the victims might have returned *alive* had a ceasefire-hostage deal been reached, while Israeli officials attribute their deaths to Hamas. Thus, the pole can accommodate both condemnation of Hamas and criticism of the Israeli government. However, because terms for negotiations or ceasefire do not dominate the loading list, **hostage-deal advocacy is best treated as a recurring extension of the core victim-and-mourning discourse rather than its defining feature**.

## Groups driving the pole

The strongest mean is **global_north_2024_09** at **-2.09**, by a substantial margin. This accords directly with the examples and with the historical event around which they cluster: the recovery of six hostages’ bodies and the funeral and memorial coverage that followed. The relatively large SD of 2.71 suggests that this discourse was especially prominent in a subset of September texts rather than uniformly distributed across that group.

Other strongly negative Global North groups include:

- **global_north_2025_01:** -1.77  
- **global_north_2023_10:** -1.66  
- **global_north_2024_10:** -1.47  
- **global_north_2025_02:** -1.44  
- **global_north_2023_11:** -1.42  

October–November 2023 plausibly reflects immediate reporting on the 7 October abductions, missing persons, Nova festival victims, and early hostage releases or memorialisation. The renewed negativity in January–February 2025 may indicate another period of intensified hostage identification, release, handover, reunion, or mourning coverage, although that interpretation remains provisional without examples from those months.

A parallel, but weaker, temporal concentration occurs in the Global South:

- **global_south_2024_09:** -1.16  
- **global_south_2025_01:** -1.13  

September 2024 and January 2025 are therefore troughs in both geopolitical divisions, suggesting that major hostage-related developments generated transnational news attention. Nevertheless, the pole is generally more strongly represented in Global North groups. Global South monthly means are usually around -0.35 to -0.79, while many Global North means fall below -1.0. This may reflect greater Global North emphasis on Israeli and Israeli-American hostage biographies, family campaigns, Jewish communal mourning, and the Nova festival narrative. It could also reflect differences in outlet composition or available article volume, so it should not be taken as a simple geopolitical opposition.

The September 2023 means are positive, but they are based on very small samples—25 Global North and 10 Global South texts—and have large SDs, especially in the Global South. They should not be used as a stable pre-conflict baseline. More generally, although the IQR cap limits dominance by the largest groups, group sizes remain uneven, so the monthly pattern may combine real historical salience with sampling and publication effects.

## Alternative labels

1. **October 7 hostage biographies and commemorative grief**  
   Emphasises the biographical and memorial treatment of abductees, though it is somewhat narrower than the full loading set.

2. **Nova festival and kibbutz captive memorialisation**  
   Captures the recurrent locations and victim profiles in the examples, but may overstate their exclusivity across the full corpus.

3. **Hostage recovery, bereavement, and bring-them-home advocacy**  
   Highlights the sequence from bodily recovery to mourning and political mobilisation. “Advocacy” is relevant in the examples but less prominent in the lexical loadings.

4. **Familial and communal mourning for Israeli captives**  
   Accurately reflects *parent, daughter, love, grief, funeral, vigil, memorial,* and *synagogue*, but underrepresents the abduction, violence, forensic recovery, and tunnel vocabulary.

Overall, **“Personalised Israeli hostage victimhood, recovery, and mourning”** is the most inclusive label because it captures the pole’s combined emphasis on captivity, violent death, bodily recovery, individual biography, family grief, and public commemoration.
