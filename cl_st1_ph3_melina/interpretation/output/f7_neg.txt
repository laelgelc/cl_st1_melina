## Factor f7 negative pole

### Recommended label
**Personalised hostage loss, public mourning, and cultural commemoration**

### Other possible labels
- **Commemorative narratives of captivity and violent death**
- **Human-interest victim profiles and collective grief**
- **Hostage memorialisation and personalised October 7 trauma**
- **Public mourning for victims of attacks on civilian leisure spaces**

## Interpretation

This pole encodes reporting that **personalises victims of kidnapping and violent death and then situates their stories within rituals of grief, remembrance, and public reaction**. In the supplied examples, it is particularly associated with Israeli hostages taken on 7 October, especially the six hostages whose bodies were recovered from a Gaza tunnel at the end of August 2024. The discourse reconstructs victims’ lives through family relationships, leisure interests, and biographical details, while narrating captivity, death, funerals, vigils, and demands for accountability or a hostage deal.

### Evidence from the loadings

The loading vocabulary comprises several closely connected lexical domains:

1. **Captivity, disappearance, and recovery**
   - *captivity, kidnap, missing, unaccounted, alive, handover, reunite, tunnel, discovery*
   
   These words construct uncertainty about hostages’ status, efforts to recover them, and the distinction between returning people alive and recovering bodies.

2. **Violent death and forensic reconstruction**
   - *grenade, explode, shooting, shot, murder, slaughter, horrific, forensic, paramedic, device*
   
   This vocabulary supports detailed reconstructions of attacks and deaths. The pole therefore involves not merely abstract conflict reporting but accounts of how identifiable individuals were injured, captured, or killed.

3. **Mourning and commemoration**
   - *vigil, grief, memorial, condolence, commemorate, commemoration, funeral, heartbroken, shatter*
   
   These are among the clearest indicators of a commemorative discourse. Death is represented through emotional response and organised public rituals rather than only through casualty statistics.

4. **Personal and familial identification**
   - *parent, daughter, girl, teenager, friend, birthday, female*
   
   Such words individualise victims through kinship, age, friendship, and life-course details. They encourage readers to encounter victims as children, parents, friends, or young people with interrupted futures.

5. **Culture, leisure, and ordinary life**
   - *festival, concert, music, song, soccer, football, athlete, beach, holiday, entertainment, documentary*
   
   This cluster is important because it places violence against the background of ordinary civilian and cultural activity. Victims are described as festival-goers, music lovers, football fans, athletes, or people celebrating birthdays. The result is a discourse of normal life violently interrupted.

6. **Public assembly and reaction**
   - *organizer, crowd, chant, barricade, riot, hurl, memorial, fundraising*
   
   These words extend the pole from private grief to collective response: vigils, funerals, memorial exhibitions, demonstrations, and sometimes confrontation surrounding such events.

The proper-name loading *Gat* is especially revealing in the examples, referring to Carmel Gat, one of the six hostages whose bodies were recovered. Together with *festival, music, grenade,* and *tunnel*, it helps explain the pole’s particularly strong activation in September 2024 reporting.

## Evidence from the examples

All ten excerpts come from **Global North September 2024**, and all focus on the six deceased hostages, especially Hersh Goldberg-Polin and Carmel Gat. They strongly instantiate the loading pattern but also show how its lexical domains combine into a recognisable discourse.

The reports repeatedly reconstruct Goldberg-Polin’s biography: he attended the Nova music *festival* with a *friend* to celebrate his *birthday*, loved *music* and *soccer*, and was advocated for by his *parents*. His injury is narrated through the *grenade* attack, his period in *captivity*, and the recovery of his body from a *tunnel*. These details turn a military event into a personalised narrative of an interrupted young life.

The commemorative component is equally prominent. The excerpts describe:

- a *vigil* at a Nova memorial exhibition;
- public *grief* and anger;
- *funerals* attended by thousands;
- official *condolences* and apologies;
- parents and political leaders asking forgiveness;
- crowds commemorating the dead and demanding the return of those still *alive*.

Several excerpts profile each hostage through ordinary interests and relationships: music, concerts, beaches, travel, work, partners, children, and friends. This directly reflects the broader leisure and family vocabulary in the loadings. The discourse thus restores individuality to people otherwise classified collectively as “hostages” or casualties.

The examples also contain moral and political evaluation—terms such as *murder, slaughter,* and *horrific*, together with apologies for failing to bring captives home. Some reports direct blame primarily at Hamas; others criticise Netanyahu for not reaching a ceasefire-hostage agreement. The common factor is not one uniform political position but the use of **personal loss and public mourning as the basis for moral judgement and political accountability**.

## Groups driving the pole

The strongest mean is for **global_north_2024_09** at **−2.277**, making it the clearest driver of this negative pole. This is strongly corroborated by the examples: every high-scoring excerpt belongs to that group, with individual scores from −11 to −15. The articles come from US, British, and Canadian sources, suggesting that the September concentration is not confined to a single national subsection of the Global North sample.

The historical trigger is apparent: the recovery of six hostages’ bodies, announced at the beginning of September 2024, generated intensive reporting on their biographies, captivity, deaths, funerals, vigils, and the resulting protests. The lexical signature of the factor is therefore especially well matched to that month.

Other relatively strong Global North groups include:

- **global_north_2023_10:** −1.994  
- **global_north_2024_10:** −1.847  
- **global_north_2023_11:** −1.779  
- **global_north_2025_01:** −1.706  
- **global_north_2025_02:** −1.577  

The October–November 2023 scores plausibly reflect immediate reporting on the Nova festival, kibbutzim, kidnappings, missing people, eyewitness accounts, funerals, and early vigils after 7 October. The renewed strength in October 2024 may reflect first-anniversary commemorations, consistent with loadings such as *one-year, commemorate, commemoration, memorial,* and *vigil*. The January–February 2025 scores may indicate renewed hostage-focused coverage, although no excerpts are supplied from those months, so that interpretation should remain cautious.

## Geopolitical and temporal tendencies

With the exception of the very small Global South September 2023 group, all group means are negative, indicating that this discourse is distributed across much of the corpus rather than being exclusive to one stratum. Nevertheless, Global North groups are consistently more negative than corresponding Global South groups. September 2024 provides the clearest comparison:

- Global North: **−2.277**
- Global South: **−1.102**

The Global South also reaches its strongest substantial negative mean in September 2024, suggesting that the recovery and death of the six hostages had cross-regional news salience. However, the markedly stronger Global North mean indicates greater concentration on this pole’s combination of hostage biography, Israeli collective grief, and commemorative reporting.

A broad temporal pattern is visible in both regions: stronger activation around the immediate aftermath of October 2023 and again around September–October 2024, with weaker scores during much of early 2024. This supports an **event-responsive and commemorative cycle**: the discourse intensifies around major hostage developments, funerals, memorial events, and anniversaries rather than developing in a simple linear direction.

These comparisons should not be treated as evidence of intrinsic regional preferences without qualification. Group sizes are uneven, and the September 2023 groups are especially small—Global North \(N=25\) and Global South \(N=10\). The Global South September 2023 positive mean of 2.5 is therefore not a reliable basis for diachronic interpretation. The stronger Global North pattern may reflect genuine editorial emphasis, the historical salience of Israeli and dual-national hostages to North American and British outlets, or the corpus’s source and sampling structure.

## Overall characterisation

The negative pole of f7 is best understood as a **personalised, affective, and commemorative discourse of hostage victimhood**. It joins three elements:

1. biographical reconstruction of victims through family and ordinary cultural life;
2. narration of abduction, captivity, injury, and violent death;
3. collective mourning through vigils, funerals, memorials, condolences, and protests.

The recommended label, **“Personalised hostage loss, public mourning, and cultural commemoration,”** captures both the full loading structure and the dominant September 2024 examples without reducing the pole solely to one incident or one named hostage.
